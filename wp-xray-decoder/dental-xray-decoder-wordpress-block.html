Dental X-ray Decoder

Bitewings, periapicals, panoramics and cone-beam CT do different jobs. See what each one captures, what it can help your dentist find, what it can't show, and how much radiation it involves.

General education, checked October 2026. This page can't read your X-ray and isn't a diagnosis.

Swelling in your face or neck, fever, or trouble swallowing or breathing? Call 911 or go to the nearest emergency room now.

Which X-ray did you have?

Pick what it was like. We'll open that X-ray below.

Bitewings and periapicals feel almost the same. The picture tells them apart: bitewings show upper and lower teeth together; periapicals show whole teeth down to the root tips.

Bitewings

2DInside the mouthDraft · clinical review

One image coversThe crowns of your upper and lower back teeth together in one image, down to about the level of the bone. 12

How it's takenA small sensor goes inside your mouth and you bite on a tab, so the upper and lower teeth are captured together. Usually two or four images, one or two per side. Vertical bitewings stand the sensor on end to show more of the bone. 13

On your plan

BWBWX2BW4BWBitewingsVertical bitewings

D0272 two imagesD0274 four imagesD0277 vertical bitewings, 7 to 8 imagesD0270 one image 4

What it shows

  • The crowns of the upper and lower back teeth
  • The contact points between neighboring teeth
  • Existing fillings and the edges of crowns
  • The top of the bone between the teeth

256

What it can help a dentist find

  • Decay between the back teeth, where it can't be seen in the mouth. The FDA calls bitewings the most efficient way to find it.
  • Whether old fillings and crowns still fit well at their edges.
  • The bone level between the teeth, which drops with gum disease. Vertical bitewings are used when more bone loss is suspected.

367

What it can't show well

  • It stops at about the level of the bone, so it doesn't show the root tips.
  • Very early decay can't be seen yet, and decay is usually further along than the X-ray suggests.
  • Decay on the chewing surface often doesn't show until it has passed through the enamel.
  • A metal filling can hide decay underneath it.
  • It doesn't show the gums, and bone loss can be greater than it appears.

256

Periapical (PA)

2DInside the mouthDraft · clinical review

One image coversOne or two whole teeth, from the crown to just past the tip of the root, with the bone around them. 12

How it's takenA small sensor is held inside your mouth, lined up with the teeth being checked. Each image covers one area, so a dentist may take several. 2

On your plan

PAPeriapicalPA filmPeriapical image

D0220 first imageD0230 each additional image 4

What it shows

  • The whole tooth, from the crown to the root tip
  • The bone around the root, and the thin space that holds the tooth in its socket
  • Nearby landmarks, such as the sinus above the upper back teeth or a small nerve opening below the lower premolars

12

What it can help a dentist find

  • Infection at the tip of a root, which can show up as a dark area.
  • Root shape, and roots that are wearing away (resorption).
  • Problems in the tooth's nerve, and injuries to teeth. The FDA notes that X-rays taken inside the mouth are more accurate for these than a panoramic.
  • Where a tooth that hasn't come in yet is sitting.
  • Bone loss and decay near the roots.

7131415

What it can't show well

  • It's a flat picture of a three-dimensional tooth, so roots and canals can overlap and hide each other.
  • It may not show how many roots or canals a tooth really has.
  • Early infection at a root tip can show on a 3D scan before it appears here.

14

Radiation
about 1.5 µSv or less per imageFigure given for a single image taken inside the mouth. 1617
How often
When there's a specific reason: pain, swelling, a deep cavity, an injury, or before root canal treatment. New patients often get selected periapicals along with bitewings. 714
Typical U.S. price, no insurance
$15–$100per image. Ranges from cost guides and financing companies; your office's fee may differ. 9101112

Panoramic (pano)

2DOutside the mouthDraft · clinical review

One image coversYour whole mouth in one wide image: all the teeth, both jaws and the surrounding structures, including the sinuses. 118

How it's takenTaken outside the mouth. You stand or sit still, bite on a small guide, and the machine's arm swings around your head. 118

On your plan

PanoPANPanoramicOPG

D0330 panoramic image 4

What it shows

  • Every tooth, including ones that haven't come in
  • The upper and lower jawbones
  • The sinuses above the upper teeth
  • The nerve canal in the lower jaw
  • The area around the jaw joints

18

What it can help a dentist find

  • Impacted teeth, including wisdom teeth.
  • Cysts and other changes in the jawbone, including tumors.
  • Advanced gum disease.
  • Jaw joint (TMJ) problems and sinus problems.
  • Planning implants, dentures, braces and extractions.
  • How a child's teeth are developing.

1718

What it can't show well

  • Less detail for each tooth. It isn't the best way to find cavities unless the decay is advanced.
  • Parts of the picture can be blurred or stretched, so exact measurements aren't possible.
  • Position matters: a small tilt of the head can distort the image.

1718

Radiation
14 to 75 µSv14 to 30 µSv with a digital (CCD) sensor, 19 to 75 µSv with a phosphor plate. RadiologyInfo gives a typical 25 µSv, about 3 days of natural background. 819
How often
When the whole mouth needs checking: wisdom teeth, growth in children and teens, or planning larger treatment. 718
Typical U.S. price, no insurance
$75–$343per image. Ranges from cost guides and financing companies; your office's fee may differ. 1020

Cone-beam CT (CBCT)

3DOutside the mouthDraft · clinical review

One image coversA 3D block of your teeth and jaws. The block can be small (about five teeth), medium (one or both jaws) or large (most of the face and skull). 1621

How it's takenTaken outside the mouth. The machine rotates around your head and builds a 3D image the dentist can slice in any direction. It isn't the same as a hospital CT scan. 2223

On your plan

CBCTCone beamCone beam CT3D scan

D0364 small field, less than one whole jawD0365 one full arch, lowerD0366 one full arch, upperD0367 both jawsD0368 jaw joints 4

What it shows

  • Teeth, roots and bone in three dimensions
  • The width and height of the jawbone
  • The path of the nerve canals
  • The sinuses
  • The bony parts of the jaw joints

2324

What it can help a dentist find

  • Planning dental implants.
  • Planning surgery for impacted teeth.
  • Complex root canal anatomy, extra canals, and suspected root cracks when regular X-rays are inconclusive.
  • Roots wearing away (resorption) and some dental injuries.
  • Jaw, sinus and jaw-joint bone problems, and planning for braces or jaw surgery.

142223

What it can't show well

  • Not for routine checks or screening. Current ADA guidance says it shouldn't be the first or only imaging.
  • Soft tissues don't show well: muscles, glands, nerves, or the disc inside the jaw joint. MRI or medical CT is used for those.
  • Metal fillings, crowns and braces can cause streaks that hide detail.
  • Moving during the scan blurs it.
  • More radiation than regular dental X-rays, so the smallest area needed should be scanned.

Full-mouth series (FMX)

A set of periapicals plus bitewings that together show the crowns and roots of every tooth and the bone around them. 237

About 35 µSv for an adult set of 18 images with a rectangular beam, up to about 171 µSv with an older round beam. 8 Typically $60 to $430 per visit for a full set or a panoramic. 91112

D0210 comprehensive series of radiographic images · On plans: FMX, Full mouth series, Full mouth X-rays, Complete series

Radiation, compared

Effective dose for adults, with a medical head CT for comparison. People in the U.S. get about 3.1 mSv (3,100 µSv) a year from natural background radiation, about 8.5 µSv a day. 1928

Ranges: 816 Doses vary with the machine, the settings and the size of the area imaged.

Good to know

X-rays should be based on need

There is no single schedule that fits everyone. The ADA's 2026 guidance with the AAOMR says X-rays should be ordered only after the dentist has looked at your medical and dental history, earlier X-rays and today's exam. 262829

Draft · clinical review

Lead aprons and thyroid collars

Since 2024 the ADA no longer recommends routine thyroid or abdominal shielding for dental X-rays, for patients of any age, including during pregnancy. Shields can block the image and lead to retakes. Some states, such as Indiana, still require shielding by law. 83031

Draft · clinical review

Pregnancy

The ADA says dental X-rays are considered safe at any stage of pregnancy. Tell your dentist if you are or might be pregnant. 2332

Draft · clinical review

Your copies

Under HIPAA you have the right to copies of your own X-rays. The office generally has 30 days to respond, can't charge a fee just for you to look at them, and can't refuse because of an unpaid bill. 33343536
